# Founding 20 — Sustainability Model

**Version:** 1.0  
**Prepared:** 12 November 2026  
**Status:** Preliminary proposal; no funding, sponsor, or partner commitment is implied.  
**Purpose:** Keep the first cohort free while establishing a repeatable, financially and operationally sustainable PMC model.

## 1. First-cohort rule

The first Founding 20 cohort is free to selected participants. No tuition, paid membership, paid tool, certification fee, or purchase requirement may be introduced for this cohort without separate explicit approval. A participant must not be disadvantaged for declining marketing, a testimonial, a sponsor introduction, or a paid service.

Sustainability should come first from a realistic operating scope, volunteer and in-kind contributions, partnerships, and measured actual costs—not speculative revenue forecasts.

## 2. Sustainability objectives

1. Preserve quality and merit-based access.
2. Avoid reliance on one individual or one sponsor.
3. Keep delivery commitments within confirmed mentor and specialist capacity.
4. Track actual cash costs and in-kind support.
5. Protect participant independence and privacy from funders and employers.
6. Build a reusable programme kit so future cohorts do not require a full redesign.

## 3. Contribution model

| Source | Potential contribution | Conditions and safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| Volunteer specialists | Workshops, clinics, project review, office hours | Written scope, availability, code of conduct, no unbounded support promise |
| University partnerships | Candidate referrals, venues, academic expertise, project briefs, evaluation support | No implied endorsement or accreditation without written agreement |
| Mentorship partnerships | Experienced practitioners supporting small participant groups | Clear time limit, conflict-of-interest rules, and participant choice |
| Corporate partnerships | Real problem statements, mock interviews, role briefings, project feedback, introductions | No access to the full cohort roster or assessment data; no influence over selection |
| Sponsorship | Cash or in-kind support for delivery, connectivity, software, or events | Sponsor recognition only as agreed; no control over curriculum, scores, graduation, or data access |
| Institutional collaboration | Research, evaluation, employment pathways, shared facilities, or grant support | Written agreement on roles, data controller/processor responsibilities, IP, and publication |
| Future premium services | Optional advanced coaching or executive development outside the free pilot | Separate programme, voluntary, approved, transparent pricing; never a condition of Founding 20 |

## 4. Volunteer and specialist operating model

Start with confirmed commitments, not a large aspirational list.

- Seek 4–5 mentors or specialists for the 20-person cohort where possible.
- Offer a bounded contribution, such as one monthly clinic or a defined number of review hours.
- Give each mentor a brief, role, expected response window, and escalation contact.
- Avoid assigning participants to mentors until the mentor confirms availability.
- Track volunteer hours and session cancellations so the next cohort is planned from actual capacity.
- Provide recognition only with the volunteer's consent.

If only two or three mentors are available, use group sessions and reduce the level of individual support promised. Do not advertise a 1:1 mentorship guarantee unless staffing is confirmed.

## 5. Partnership propositions

### Universities

Offer a practical employability and professional evidence pilot: structured projects, skills baselines, portfolio outputs, and aggregate impact reporting. Ask for referral channels, expert reviewers, and access to relevant facilities or sessions. Do not imply that the programme awards university credit.

### Employers and healthcare/technology organisations

Offer project briefs, specialist clinics, mock interviews, career talks, and opt-in introductions. Employer involvement should help participants understand real work and opportunities, not turn the cohort into an unpaid recruitment database.

### Professional associations and communities

Offer expert sessions, candidate referral, peer learning, and subject-matter clinics. Preserve selection independence and the community's own programme identity.

### Sponsors

Offer a transparent sponsorship menu: programme delivery support, learning resources, event support, connectivity, or impact evaluation. Report aggregate outcomes only. The sponsor should not receive participant names, emails, CVs, scores, or individual CVI unless a specific participant separately opts into a defined introduction.

## 6. Cost model and financial controls

Build a basic actual-cost ledger before setting a future cohort budget.

| Cost category | Examples | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Technology | Hosting, storage, email, analytics, domain, backups | Prefer current infrastructure and free tiers only where reliability and privacy permit |
| Delivery | Event tools, session recording, materials | Use existing approved tools; record actual consumption |
| People | Specialist honoraria, external review, programme coordination | Confirm scope and quote before committing |
| Participant access | Connectivity support, accessibility, materials | Offer fair access; do not assume all participants have identical resources |
| Evaluation | Independent assessment, reporting, follow-up | Budget based on actual reporting burden and required verification |
| Compliance | Privacy review, contracts, data-transfer assessment, security work | Do not remove necessary privacy work to meet a zero-cost target |

For every expense, record amount, currency, date, purpose, payer, approval, and whether it is cash or in-kind. Do not publish an estimated monetary value for volunteer or sponsor contributions as if it were cash revenue.

Recommended internal indicators:
- Actual cash cost per activated participant.
- Actual cash cost per graduate.
- Volunteer/specialist hours per participant.
- Percentage of planned sessions delivered.
- Confirmed in-kind resources available for the next cohort.
- Funding concentration by source.
- Participant satisfaction and outcome evidence coverage.

No revenue, cost-saving, employment, or sponsor value forecast should be represented as fact without a dated source and a stated method.

## 7. Scaling without redesign

Scale in stages:

**Stage 1 — Founding 20:** Validate selection, IDPs, project review, CVI, privacy, and actual effort with one cohort.

**Stage 2 — Repeatable cohorts:** Reuse the same rubrics and programme playbook. Increase cohort size only when mentor capacity, programme lead time, privacy controls, and impact dashboard quality are demonstrated.

**Stage 3 — Partner-supported pathways:** Add sector-specific cohorts or specialist tracks only when a partner and delivery capacity are confirmed.

**Stage 4 — Institutional model:** Consider funded cohorts, grants, corporate sponsorship, or optional premium services after the first cohort's independent retrospective and owner approval.

The data model should separate cohort metadata, participant identity mapping, pseudonymous activity, and impact measures so multiple cohorts can be compared without exposing identities. The actual database implementation should remain small and use the existing PMC CRM and Auth foundation.

## 8. Sponsor and partner ethics

- Sponsors cannot buy participant selection, graduation, or access to personal data.
- Employers receive no roster or CV bundle by default.
- Participant introductions are opt-in, specific, and revocable where applicable.
- A sponsor may fund a cohort without being allowed to dictate who benefits.
- PMC controls the selection rubric and impact definitions.
- Publish limitations and negative results as well as positive outcomes.
- Avoid claims of formal partnership, accreditation, placement, or endorsement until a signed agreement and the relevant operational capability exist.

## 9. Sustainability decision gates

Before committing to the next cohort, review:

1. Did the first cohort achieve meaningful development outcomes, not just engagement?
2. How many participants completed the graduation standard?
3. What was the actual cash cost and staff/volunteer effort?
4. Which partner commitments were delivered, and which were only proposed?
5. Were privacy and consent controls workable?
6. Are mentors and reviewers available for the next cohort?
7. Can the programme lead support the proposed scale without quality loss?
8. Is future funding sufficient without introducing participant fees that have not been approved?

The first cohort is a proof-of-model exercise. Do not scale volume until the programme's quality, data integrity, delivery capacity, and sustainability are demonstrated.
